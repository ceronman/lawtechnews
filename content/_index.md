### [Trump advisers tell AI firms they will not safety-test open-weight models](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/meta-anthropic-google-openai-meet-with-trump-white-house-amid-rogue-ai-agent-2026-08-04/)

<span class="news-item__source">reuters.com</span> The Trump administration told AI developers on 4 August that open-weight models — including Nvidia's Nemotron and Meta's Llama — will be excluded from the voluntary safety testing regime announced in a 2 June executive order, according to sources briefed on a meeting between the Office of the National Cyber Director and staff from Meta, Anthropic, Google, Nvidia and OpenAI. The framework was finalised by its 1 August deadline but has not been published, and it is unclear whether it will be; Americans for Responsible Innovation warned that sharing it with a small group of companies "deepens a serious gap in federal oversight." The meeting followed disclosures that OpenAI's and Anthropic's models breached other companies' systems — with OpenAI reporting a further incident the same day — and five Democratic senators wrote to Trump urging binding legislation for frontier models, arguing the US cannot leave its most advanced systems under "opaque, case-by-case restrictions" while Chinese alternatives look cheaper and more predictable. Primary source: the framework itself is unpublished; the underlying instrument is the 2 June 2026 executive order establishing voluntary frontier-model testing, and the finalisation was [first reported by POLITICO on 3 August](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/08/03/white-house-finalizes-voluntary-ai-oversight-framework-01022437). Useful as the transatlantic contrast to the EU AI Act's binding GPAI obligations, which entered enforcement on 2 August — a comparison worth having ready for any AI regulatory affairs interview.

### [The "Chat Control 1.0" saga: Big Tech can scan our private messages again – but Parliament sent a strong signal against mass surveillance](https://edri.org/our-work/the-chat-control-1-0-saga-big-tech-can-scan-our-private-messages-again-but-parliament-sent-a-strong-signal-against-mass-surveillance/)

<span class="news-item__source">edri.org</span> EDRi's post-mortem on the third and final vote on the interim ePrivacy derogation ("Chat Control 1.0"), which the European Parliament rejected in March 2026 — letting the 2021 derogation lapse on 4 April — only for EP President Roberta Metsola to invite the Council to push the text back through a rarely used second-reading procedure. EDRi explains the procedural mechanics that decided the outcome: in a second reading the text is adopted unless rejected, amendments need an absolute majority (360 votes) rather than a simple majority, and the EPP added an urgency procedure to bypass the LIBE committee and put the vote on the agenda of the last plenary before the summer break, when 100+ MEPs were absent — so non-voters effectively counted in favour. The derogation passed and applies from 3 August 2026 to 3 April 2028, but MEPs did carry two amendments (AM30 and PC3) protecting end-to-end encrypted interpersonal communications, including against client-side scanning, which forced a Commission opinion and Council acceptance before adoption. EDRi reads the result as a net win for the parallel and far more consequential CSA Regulation ("Chat Control 2.0"): a majority of MEPs present voted to reject and amend, signalling to trilogue negotiators — next round scheduled 29 September — that there is no majority for mass scanning, with encryption protection and the removal of age verification already agreed and only the detection model (mandatory/voluntary, targeted/mass) left open. Primary source: [Regulation published in OJ L, 2026/1881 (EUR-Lex)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ:L_202601881), and the [text as amended, P10_TA(2026)0266](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/TA-10-2026-0266_EN.html). Squarely in Maria's ePrivacy/digital rights territory and one of the files most likely to come up in EDPS, EDPB, LIBE or civil society interviews this autumn.

### [Does the EU's Special Panel Succeed in Charting a Third Path for Child Safety?](https://www.techpolicy.press/does-the-eus-special-panel-succeed-in-charting-a-third-path-for-child-safety/)

<span class="news-item__source">techpolicy.press</span> Christian Cirhigiri and Marie Seck of CDT Europe assess the co-chairs' report of the Commission's Special Panel on the protection of minors online, which is meant to give the EU a unified position as France, Austria, Greece and Spain each pursue their own bans. They welcome the report's children's-rights framing, its safety-by-design emphasis and its "reversal of proof" placing the burden on platforms, but identify three problems. First, safety by design is undefined and unoperationalised: the report never says who sets the bar for "safe" or what safety entails, and handing that to a regulator means constantly updated technical standards plus broad access restrictions in the meantime. Second, the headline recommendation — a harmonised EU-wide access restriction for under-13s, with feature-specific limits above 13 — rests on a working definition of "social media+" that exists in no legislation, creating scope uncertainty with real consequences for freedom of expression, access to information, privacy and data protection. Third, age-specific restrictions de facto mandate age verification at scale, and the report's reliance on the EU Digital Identity Wallet as the solution reopens accessibility, security and privacy concerns civil society has already raised. Their broader point: DSA Article 28 guidelines and the forthcoming Digital Fairness Act should be allowed to work before new legislation is drafted, and they flag the Parliament's recent procedural shortcuts on child-protection files as a warning about how the September debate may be run. Primary source: [European Commission — Special Panel on the protection of minors online](https://commission.europa.eu/topics/digital-economy-and-society/special-panel_en). Relevant to DSA enforcement, age verification and digital identity work at the Commission, ACM, the AP or platform-regulation NGOs.

### [Can we solve the cookie banner problem in the EU?](https://iapp.org/news/a/can-we-solve-the-cookie-banner-problem-in-the-eu-)

<span class="news-item__source">iapp.org</span> Sebastian Zimmeck (Wesleyan University, founder of the Global Privacy Control) argues that the upcoming Digital Omnibus trilogue is the EU's real chance to end consent fatigue, and that the vehicle is the proposed new GDPR Article 88b — which would let people express privacy choices automatically through technical means such as a browser setting rather than clicking through banners. He points to the Global Privacy Control as a working precedent: adopted in California, Colorado, Connecticut and other US states, implemented on at least 385,000 websites, and mandatory as a browser setting under California law from next year. The Council removed Article 88b from its position in June 2026 after lobbying from media and advertising groups claiming a third of ad revenue at risk; Zimmeck counters that targeted ads lift publisher revenue only around 4% over contextual ads, that no GPC state has seen ad-ecosystem disruption, and that browser-level consent would raise US consumer welfare by roughly $4.60 per user-week. He concedes the GPC as specified cannot express opt-in consent — but argues Article 88b does not require the same mechanism for granting and refusing, that EU authorities should map GPC to refusal and withdrawal of consent plus objection to cross-context targeting (at minimum barring banners for those purposes), and that the W3C editors would evolve the spec if the law required it. Primary source: [COM(2025) 837, the Digital Omnibus proposal amending the GDPR, ePrivacy Directive and related acts (EUR-Lex)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A52025PC0837). Concrete, technically literate ePrivacy/GDPR material on a file still in trilogue — the kind of thing that plays well in privacy counsel and DPA policy interviews.

### [UBS fined $125 million by US regulators for money laundering violations](https://www.reuters.com/world/us-treasury-fines-ubs-financial-services-125-million-secrecy-violations-2026-08-03/)

<span class="news-item__source">reuters.com</span> FinCEN assessed a $125m civil money penalty against UBS Financial Services on 3 August — the largest ever against a broker-dealer under the Bank Secrecy Act — with UBSFS admitting willful failure to implement and maintain an AML programme and to file suspicious activity reports between January 2019 and June 2023. The penalty reflects recidivism: the firm never fixed the deficiencies behind a $14.5m FinCEN fine in December 2018. Regulators cited inadequate customer due diligence on high-risk clients with Russian and Latin American ties (including a Putin-linked oligarch whose accounts persisted despite press reporting on his wealth and links to Iranian digital assets), and failure to monitor over 60,000 foreign-currency wires totalling more than $10bn. The settlement resolves parallel SEC, CFTC and FINRA actions and requires an outside consultant to review the AML programme against priority illicit-finance risks (Southwest border, cartels, narcotics, Iran, Russia, Venezuela). Primary source: [FinCEN press release and consent order, *In the Matter of UBS Financial Services Inc.*](https://www.fincen.gov/news/news-releases/fincen-assesses-historic-125-million-penalty-against-ubs-financial-services-inc). Directly relevant to Maria's AML/KYC track: a textbook case study in remediation failure, repeat-offender exposure and enhanced due diligence on PEPs and sanctions-adjacent clients — the substance of Amsterdam bank and fintech compliance interviews.

### [EU AI Act Transparency Rules Are Now In Effect. Was It A Missed Opportunity?](https://www.techpolicy.press/eu-ai-act-transparency-rules-are-now-in-effect-was-it-a-missed-opportunity/)

<span class="news-item__source">techpolicy.press</span> Two WITNESS policy specialists give the first substantive critique of the Article 50 transparency regime that took effect on 2 August, and of the Code of Practice and guidelines built around it. They credit the Code for its multi-layered marking approach (signed metadata plus watermarking), free privacy-compliant detection tools, interoperability standards and binding accessibility of disclosures — but identify four gaps: provenance information (Measure 1.3) is only "encouraged" rather than required, so all that is mandated is a binary "AI-generated" flag; open-weight models rely on voluntary cooperation, and marking cannot be enforced once weights are released and fine-tuned; public reporting and civil society oversight are thin; and labels are easily stripped by editing and re-encoding. On the guidelines they flag vagueness in the "obviousness" exception and in the line between exempt "standard editing" and regulated "substantial semantic alteration"; an internal contradiction (the guidelines concede universal watermarking and detection do not yet exist, especially for text, while the Code demands "effective, interoperable and reliable" machine-readable marking); and a personal/non-professional-use exemption that lifts the labelling duty from exactly the privately made viral deepfakes that cause most harm. They also stress that for non-consensual intimate imagery a label is not protection — recourse lies in the DSA and Directive (EU) 2024/1385 on combating violence against women. Primary source: this Tech Policy Press perspective by Bruna Santos and Jacobo Castellanos, read alongside the [Commission's Article 50 guidelines and Code of Practice](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/guidelines-transparency-ai-generated-content). The best critical counterweight to the Commission's own framing of the 2 August milestone, and useful material for showing she can identify compliance gaps rather than recite obligations.

### [EDPB requests review of EU-US Data Privacy Framework following Trump v. Slaughter](https://iapp.org/news/a/edpb-requests-review-of-eu-us-data-privacy-framework-following-trump-v-slaughter)

<span class="news-item__source">iapp.org</span> The EDPB has formally asked the European Commission to "closely assess" what the US Supreme Court's *Trump v. Slaughter* ruling on presidential removal power over independent agencies means for the EU-US Data Privacy Framework. In a 31 July letter from Chair Anu Talus to Commissioner Michael McGrath (Democracy, Justice, Rule of Law and Consumer Protection), the Board stresses that the existence and effective functioning of independent supervisory authorities in the third country is a key element of any adequacy assessment — and notes the DPF's explicit reliance on FTC independence and the "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office" removal standard that Trump bypassed in dismissing Commissioner Rebecca Kelly Slaughter. Reactions split: Max Schrems (NOYB) says "the deal is done" and is preparing a fresh CJEU challenge, arguing the Commission is a political rather than legal body and will keep the issue "under the rug"; former FTC privacy division head Maneesha Mithal argues the FTC has shown no retreat from its enforcement commitments and that SCCs and BCRs remain unaffected. Primary source: the EDPB letter of 31 July 2026 to Commissioner McGrath, reported here by IAPP's Joe Duball (the letter is not yet posted on the EDPB documents portal). This is the most consequential open question in Maria's international-transfers specialisation right now: a third Schrems-style invalidation would reshape every EU-US transfer assessment, and it is exactly the issue DPO and privacy counsel interviews will test.
